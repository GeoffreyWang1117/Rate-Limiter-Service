LLM Admission Gateway
Simulator Measured Design Source
Admission control · Redis · Lua · TypeScript

Rate limiting stops working when a request’s cost is unknown.

A conventional limiter charges one unit per request and decides once, on arrival. For LLM inference neither half holds: cost is tokens, varying by three orders of magnitude between a one-line completion and a 200k-token context — and the larger half of that cost, the output, is not known until generation stops.

So admission cannot be one decision. It is a protocol: hold budget against an unknown, reconcile when it becomes known, and reclaim when the holder never comes back. The third part is what makes it a distributed systems problem rather than bookkeeping.
The protocol

Three phases, one atomic script each

Refill, compare, deduct and lease happen inside a single Lua script, so N gateway replicas cannot over-admit by a factor of N. Redis runs a script to completion, which serialises the whole decision on the shard without any lock the gateway has to hold, renew, or lose.

01 — RESERVE

Hold the unknown

Deduct an estimate, write a lease with a deadline, return a reservation id. Under worst_case the estimate is the declared ceiling; under adaptive it is an EWMA of what this model has actually been producing.

02 — COMMIT

Reconcile

Generation finished, so the real cost is known. Refund the difference, or charge the overrun. Idempotent, because this call happens right after a slow generation — exactly when clients time out and retry.

03 — EXPIRE

Reclaim

A client that dies between the two calls would otherwise burn its tenant’s budget forever, with every individual decision still correct and nothing to alert on. Expired leases are swept in bounded batches, on the admission path.

Live instrument

Drive the protocol

Send requests and watch the budget split into free, held and spent. Complete one to see the hold reconciled. Abandon one to simulate a client crash — the budget stays held until the lease expires, and the next admission reclaims it.

Policy
What this is. A faithful browser reimplementation of the admission logic in src/llm/admission-scripts.ts — the same constraint ordering, fractional token-bucket refill, bounded inline lease sweep, idempotent settlement and EWMA estimator. It is not the service: there is no Redis, no PostgreSQL and no network here, so nothing on this page is a latency measurement. Real numbers are below, and they come from committed artifacts. Two deliberate differences: the lease default is 8 s rather than 120 s and the budget is scaled down, both so that expiry and refill are observable at human speed; and this simulator applies refill on commit, which the production Lua currently does not — a defect recorded in the engineering guide, §17.1.

The last row is the honest reading: once callers declare close to what they use, adaptive buys nothing and its overrun risk is not worth carrying.

worst_case admitting exactly 75 at every median is the self-consistency check: that mode reserves the declared ceiling and ignores what is generated, so its count cannot depend on the output distribution. An earlier version of this benchmark showed it varying from 42 down to 0 — which is how the calibration phase was found to be draining the budget before the measured burst began.

Decisions

Why it is built this way

Expiry is swept inline, not by a background job

A sweeper is the obvious design and the wrong one: another process to deploy, monitor and elect a leader for, returning budget on its own schedule instead of when someone needs it — and it introduces the one race the protocol most needs to avoid, refunding a hold at the moment its owner commits it. A bounded sweep at the top of the admit script has none of those properties and needs no coordination.

The balance may go negative

An overrun is charged as a negative refund and the tenant refills out of the hole. Clamping at zero would make tokens beyond the reservation free, turning underestimation into a strategy and making adaptive mode exploitable by design rather than merely imprecise.

Commit is idempotent; reserve is not

Commit is called by a client that just waited out a slow generation, which is exactly when its request times out and gets retried. A retried reserve, by contrast, is a genuinely new request for capacity — the first is released by its lease, and deduplicating it would need an idempotency key the caller has no reason to have.

The two failure modes differ

The generic limiter fails open; LLM admission fails closed. A limiter that returns 500 on a Redis blip has made its own availability the ceiling on everything it fronts. But unmetered traffic into a fixed accelerator pool does not degrade gracefully — it queues until the backend collapses and every in-flight request is lost, not just the excess.

Known limits

  • One process, one core. Capacity comes from replicas; the gateway is stateless and every decision is one atomic script.
  • No production trace. Output lengths are drawn from an assumed distribution, which is why the reservation result is a sweep rather than a number.
  • No LLM is behind the gateway in any benchmark. Admission decides before generation starts; the measurements cover the decision and the accounting, not end-to-end serving.
  • Redis Cluster has never been deployed. Keys carry hash tags so multi-key scripts stay in one slot, but that is a claim about key layout, not a tested deployment.
  • Three defects are open and documented, including a missing refill on the commit path. They are recorded rather than quietly fixed, with reproductions.
